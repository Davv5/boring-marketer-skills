# AI Tells

The patterns that mark text as AI-written. Readers spot them and stop trusting the copy. Use this list as an editing pass over a finished draft: find every tell, then rewrite the sentence so it sounds like a specific person writing from real experience. The pass is done when the checklist at the end holds for the whole draft.

## Words

- delve, dive into, dig into
- comprehensive, robust, cutting-edge
- utilize (write "use")
- leverage (as a verb)
- crucial, vital, essential
- unlock, unleash, supercharge
- game-changer, revolutionary
- landscape, navigate, streamline
- tapestry, multifaceted, myriad
- foster, facilitate, enhance
- realm, paradigm, synergy
- embark, journey (for processes)
- plethora, bevy
- nuanced, intricate, seamless

## Phrases

- "In today's fast-paced world..." / "In today's digital age..."
- "It's important to note that..."
- "When it comes to..."
- "In order to..." (write "to")
- "Whether you're a... or a..."
- "Are you ready to take your X to the next level?"
- "Are you looking for..." and other fake questions
- "Look no further"
- "Let's dive in" / "Let's explore" / "Without further ado"
- "At the end of the day"
- "It goes without saying"
- "In conclusion", especially at the end
- "This comprehensive guide will..."

## Punctuation

- More than one or two em-dashes in a piece (use periods)
- Sentences with four or more commas (break them up)
- Colons in title after title
- Semicolons where a period would work

## Structure

- **Triple pattern**: everything in threes (three benefits, three examples, three subpoints). Humans are messier.
- **Perfect parallelism**: every paragraph or bullet the same length and shape, every bullet starting the same way.
- **Hedge stack**: "While X, it's important to consider Y, but also Z." Never commits.
- **Fake objectivity**: "Some experts say... others believe..." without taking a position.
- **Summary sandwich**: the intro summarises, the body covers, the conclusion summarises again.
- **Empty transitions**: "Now that we've covered X, let's move on to Y."
- **Over-formatting**: too many headings, bold on every key term, numbered lists where the order doesn't matter.

## Voice

- **No opinions**: everything balanced, nothing claimed. Real experts have takes.
- **No mistakes mentioned**: never wrong about anything.
- **Hedging**: "some may find", "it's possible that", "can potentially".
- **Uniform certainty**: every statement equally confident. People commit where sure and hedge where unsure.
- **Generic examples**: "a business might..." instead of a real story.
- **Distance from the subject**: writing about it, not from experience of it.
- **Lifeless correctness**: passive voice throughout, no contractions, no "I" or "you", perfectly grammatical and flat.

## The fix

Real people use contractions, write the odd fragment, state opinions without hedging, say "I" and "you" freely, and make unexpected word choices. Read the draft out loud: where you stumble, a reader will too; where it sounds like a textbook, rewrite it.

## Checklist

- [ ] No tell from the Words or Phrases lists
- [ ] Not everything in threes
- [ ] At least one opinion stated directly
- [ ] At least one specific number from real experience
- [ ] At least one admission of a limitation or uncertainty
- [ ] Sentence lengths vary (some under 5 words, some over 20)
- [ ] It sounds like one identifiable person, not a committee: you would say it out loud to a smart friend
